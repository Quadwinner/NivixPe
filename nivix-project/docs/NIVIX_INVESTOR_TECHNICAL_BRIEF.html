1. Executive Summary

Nivix is a cross-border payments platform that settles international transfers in seconds instead of days, at a cost of under $1 instead of the $25–45 typically charged by bank wires. It does this by using USDC — a fully regulated, dollar-backed stablecoin issued by Circle — as the settlement layer between two licensed local payout partners, one in the sender's country and one in the recipient's.

Two things make this legally and technically sound, and both are deliberate design choices, not accidents:

  • Nivix never issues its own token. Every dollar of value on-chain is real, audited USDC. This avoids the unlicensed-stablecoin problem that sinks most "crypto remittance" projects.
  • Nivix operates under GIFT City IFSC, a jurisdiction India's own regulator (IFSCA) treats as a non-resident zone for foreign-exchange law. This is what makes a stablecoin-settled corridor legal for us when it would not be legal from mainland India directly.
The one-line pitch: Licensed fiat partners collect and pay out local currency on each end. We hold our own pre-funded USDC treasury in the middle and move it on Solana in about two seconds. We own the treasury, the routing logic, and the compliance ledger — the pieces that create defensibility and margin.

This document has two audiences in mind. If you are evaluating the business, Sections 1–3 and 6–10 are the ones that matter. If you or your technical advisor want to verify the engineering underneath the pitch, Sections 4, 5, and Appendix A go deep enough to do that. Section 5 in particular corrects a simplification from our earlier investor memo — we'd rather you hear the precise version from us than discover the gap yourselves.

2. The Problem

Cross-border retail and SME payments still run on infrastructure designed in the 1970s (SWIFT correspondent banking):

MetricTraditional banking / SWIFTModern fintech (Wise, Remitly)
Settlement time3–5 business days1–2 days (still batches through banking hours)
Fee on a $100 transfer$25–45$5–15
TransparencyOpaque — no visibility into where funds sit mid-transferPartial tracking, still opaque internally
AvailabilityBanking hours only24/7 front-end, but still bank-hour settlement underneath

The bottleneck is always the same: money has to physically move between correspondent banks, each taking a cut and a day. Stablecoins solve the movement problem — a USDC transfer settles on Solana in about two seconds for a fraction of a cent — but only if the fiat legs on either end are handled by properly licensed entities. That licensing question, not the technology, is why most "crypto for remittance" projects stall. It's also why we built the legal structure (Section 6) before the payment rails.

3.3 Why a treasury instead of a pass-through

An alternative design would have Nivix simply forward whatever the on-ramp partner sends, without holding its own balance. We rejected that:

No treasury (pass-through only)Own treasury (our choice)
Settlement waits on the partner's own conversion speedUSDC is already in our wallet — release is instant
We don't control the spreadWe control the full margin on both legs
A partner outage stalls the whole transferTreasury float gives us buffer and independence

3.4 Keeping the treasury funded

The treasury is a float, not a per-transaction top-up:

TWO-WAY CORRIDOR (India ⇄ USA):
  India→USA leg:  ₹ in  (treasury's INR side grows)  |  USDC out (treasury shrinks)
  USA→India leg:  $ in via reverse flow (USDC side grows) |  ₹ out (INR side shrinks)
  NET EFFECT: balanced two-way traffic self-balances the treasury automatically.

ONE-SIDED CORRIDOR (e.g. 90% India→USA, 10% reverse):
  Accumulated INR is periodically converted to USDC (weekly/monthly, not per-transfer)
  Auto-trigger: buy USDC when treasury capacity < 30%.  Alert ops if < 10%.

4. Technical Architecture

This section is for readers (or your technical advisors) who want to verify the engineering, not just the pitch.

4.1 Why two blockchains, not one

ConcernChain usedWhy not the other one
Money settlement (must be publicly verifiable)Solana — USDC transfers, treasury balance, proof of reservesA private ledger can't prove solvency to the outside world; a public one can
Compliance records (KYC, PII, risk decisions)Hyperledger Fabric, multi-organisationPersonal identity data cannot legally sit on a public chain anyone can read
The fraud-safety property that matters to a diligence team: Fabric is deployed multi-org (Nivix + an independent endorsing organisation). Every KYC write requires endorsement from both organisations before it commits. Nivix alone cannot alter or fabricate a compliance record — the same class of control failure that sank FTX is structurally closed off here, because no single party controls the ledger.

5. Settlement Reality: What Circle Actually Does

This section exists because an earlier internal explanation of the off-ramp leg was oversimplified. We're correcting it here deliberately, because it's exactly the kind of gap a technical due-diligence reviewer would probe for — better it comes from us.

The simplification we're correcting: Our July memo described "Circle Mint" as the off-ramp partner that pays the recipient directly. Having read Circle's own API documentation closely, that is not what Circle Mint does.

5.1 What Circle Mint actually is

Circle Mint lets an approved business redeem its own USDC 1:1 for real US dollars (or EURC for euros), wired into a bank account that business has already pre-registered with Circle. Circle's own quickstart guide shows the redemption call requires a destination object referencing an account ID created in an earlier step — there is no field for an arbitrary third party's bank details:

"destination": {
  "type": "wire",
  "id": "9d1fa351-b24d-442a-8aa5-e717db1ed636"   ← must be OUR pre-linked account
}

In other words: Circle Mint turns our own USDC into our own dollars. It is treasury infrastructure for Nivix, not a payout API to Nivix's customers. Access also requires Circle's own KYB/sanctions review (days to weeks) and is limited to wire/SEPA rails at participating banks in eligible regions.

5.2 The corrected model: two hops, not one

The international leg of a transfer therefore needs two separate, licensed steps, not one:

Why this is actually a strength, not a weakness: no single partner ever touches both the crypto leg and the fiat-to-recipient leg. That separation is a compliance feature — it mirrors the same multi-party control principle we apply on the Fabric side (Section 4.1), just applied to the money rails instead of the ledger.

5.3 What's already proven on the India side

On the India (INR) side, this two-hop concern doesn't arise the same way: our current integration with Cashfree Payouts bundles both jobs into a single API — it holds settlement funds and pays named beneficiaries directly (bank transfer, or a claim-link "Cashgram" the recipient redeems to a bank account or UPI of their choice). This is the flow already demonstrated end-to-end in our proof-of-concept: sender pays INR → on-chain settlement → recipient receives INR in their own account.

What remains open is the equivalent last-mile partner for each destination country — that integration work is scoped as Phase 2 (Milestone M2/E5) of the build plan and has not started yet. It is a scope item, not a design flaw: the treasury, the Solana settlement rail, and the compliance ledger are all corridor-agnostic and don't change when we add a country.

5.4 How to say this to a technical reviewer

"Circle Mint is our treasury conversion layer — it turns our own USDC into real, regulated dollars in our own account, 1:1. The last mile — actually paying the named recipient — is a separate, licensed payout integration per corridor: Cashfree today in India, and an equivalent partner (Wise Platform, Nium, or a direct bank wire/ACH API) for each new country we add. Two regulated hops, each auditable independently."

This is a regulatory pathway, not a loophole for mainland retail INR flows — money originating or terminating outside the IFSC still needs the standard LRS/AD-bank route or a FIU-registered VDASP partner for its domestic leg.

11. Investor FAQ

Where does the money actually come from?

From our pre-funded USDC treasury. When a sender pays local currency, that currency lands in our bank account via the on-ramp partner. We release the equivalent USDC from our own Solana treasury. The treasury refills from two-way traffic plus periodic conversion of accumulated fiat — see Section 3.4.

What does Nivix actually own and control?

The USDC treasury, the orchestration and routing layer, and the compliance engine (the Hyperledger audit trail). We do not hold customer fiat directly beyond a payment's confirmation webhook, we do not issue our own token, and we do not carry unhedged FX risk.

How do you make money?

Platform fee (0.3–0.5%) plus FX spread (0.2–0.5%) plus treasury yield on idle USDC. See Section 7 for the full model and illustrative economics.

Why Solana instead of just using SWIFT?

Speed (seconds versus days), cost (fractions of a cent versus $25–45), and a publicly verifiable settlement trail instead of opaque internal bank records — and it runs 24/7, not on banking hours.

Why Hyperledger as well as Solana?

They do different jobs. Solana is public — it's how anyone can verify our treasury reserves and settlement history. Hyperledger is private — it's where KYC and personal data live, visible only to authorized parties. Public money movement plus private compliance is the combination that lets us be both transparent and legally compliant with data protection law.

Does Circle handle the whole off-ramp?

No — see Section 5. Circle Mint converts our own USDC into our own fiat. Actually paying the named recipient is a second, separate licensed integration per corridor (Cashfree today in India; an equivalent partner needed for each new country).

Do you need to refill the treasury before every transaction?

No. Two-way corridors self-balance automatically. One-sided corridors are rebalanced periodically (weekly/monthly), with automatic threshold alerts — never per-transaction.

What if USDC loses its peg?

USDC is the most heavily regulated major stablecoin — backed 1:1 by US Treasuries and cash, audited monthly. A de-peg event would be an industry-wide shock, not a Nivix-specific one. We monitor continuously and can pause settlement instantly if needed.

What is the moat here?

First-mover regulatory positioning in GIFT City for stablecoin-settled remittance; a dual-blockchain compliance design that is hard to replicate quickly and that regulators respond well to; a treasury model that gives genuinely instant settlement instead of waiting on a partner's own conversion speed; and a routing engine designed from day one to add corridors without re-architecting.

Appendix B — Glossary

USDC
A stablecoin issued by Circle, redeemable 1:1 for US dollars and backed by cash and short-term US Treasuries.
Solana
A public blockchain used here purely as a fast, cheap settlement rail for USDC — not for anything requiring privacy.
Hyperledger Fabric
A private, permissioned blockchain framework used for the compliance ledger. "Multi-org" means more than one independent organisation must approve (endorse) each write.
On-ramp / off-ramp
The fiat entry and exit points of a transfer — the licensed local partners who collect and pay out local currency.
IFSC / IFSCA
India's International Financial Services Centre (GIFT City, Gujarat) and its regulator, the International Financial Services Centres Authority — a jurisdiction treated as non-resident for Indian foreign-exchange law.
Circle Mint
Circle's business product for redeeming USDC/EURC 1:1 into a pre-registered bank account belonging to the redeeming business (see Section 5).
KYC / KYB
Know Your Customer / Know Your Business — identity and entity verification required before onboarding.
Endorsement (Fabric)
The requirement that a transaction be approved by specified organisations' peers before it is committed to the ledger.
