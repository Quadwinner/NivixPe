3. The One Rule That Fixes Most of It

Stablecoins never touch a customer. Not bought, not held, not burned. Customers only ever see fiat — they pay fiat in, and a recipient receives fiat out. Any stablecoin in the system exists purely as Nivix's own internal treasury tool, moved only between Nivix's own custody and a licensed partner's custody.

This single rule does most of the legal work in this document:

  • It removes retail crypto custody entirely — the single highest-scrutiny risk category in row 2 of Section 2 — because there is no customer-held asset to regulate.
  • It removes the self-issued-token problem structurally (row 1) — there's no token for a customer to receive, so there's nothing to mislabel as a currency.
  • It reframes the customer's payment as a domestic transaction for a service, not a crypto purchase (row 3) — though the precise legal weight of that reframing for India specifically still needs confirmation; see Section 5.

5. Legal Basis — Leg A: India Collection

5.1 The regulatory reality, plainly

  • RBI does not recognize crypto or stablecoins as legal tender or as a permitted remittance instrument.
  • FEMA's Liberalized Remittance Scheme (LRS) explicitly excludes purchasing virtual digital assets as a permitted purpose for outward remittance.
  • Any entity dealing in virtual digital assets must register with FIU-IND as a reporting entity under PMLA — this is an anti-money-laundering obligation, not a license to run a VDA remittance business.

5.2 What this architecture deliberately does NOT do

It does not let a mainland Indian customer's remittance instruction convert into crypto. The customer pays a licensed domestic partner in INR for a payment service — a domestic transaction. Whatever happens next (Nivix's own treasury operations, Leg B) is Nivix managing its own working capital, using its own money, to fulfil that service abroad.

5.3 Why this still needs sign-off — the single most important open question VERIFY

The pattern "customer pays for a service domestically; the provider separately settles internationally using its own assets" is an established one in payments — it is roughly how many money transfer operators net-settle across borders without physically moving the customer's own currency. But whether Nivix's specific structure satisfies this in substance is not something this document can certify:

  • Does an IFSC-based entity receiving a mainland customer's payment through a domestic collection partner actually get FEMA's "non-resident jurisdiction" treatment for this specific flow, or does it still require the collection leg itself to hold a specific RBI-recognized cross-border remittance authorization?
  • Candidate categories worth researching with counsel: status as an Authorised Dealer, or authorization under RBI's Payment Aggregator–Cross Border framework VERIFY exact current name/requirements.
This is exactly what the "confirm IFSCA sandbox scope with the lawyer" item already flagged for this project in earlier planning. This document does not resolve that question — the rest of this architecture depends on its answer.

6. Legal Basis — Leg B: Treasury & Custody

Once fiat has been collected domestically for a service (Leg A), Nivix's treasury is Nivix's own money — the same as any company's working capital. The relevant legal question shifts from "is this remittance" to "is this money safeguarded properly":

RequirementWhy it matters
Funds purchased through a regulated, KYB'd channel (a licensed exchange, or an institutional product like Circle Mint) — never self-mintedEstablishes that the USDC in the treasury is real and traceable to a legitimate purchase, not fabricated

7. Legal Basis — Leg C: Destination Country & Licensing Strategy

7.1 Why USDC specifically

In the US, the GENIUS Act VERIFY current status/requirements established a federal framework for "payment stablecoins" — issuers must hold 1:1 reserves in cash and short-term Treasuries, and undergo regular audits. This is what makes Circle's USDC a compliant settlement asset to route through, as opposed to an unregulated token — the same distinction that rules out any self-issued asset in this design.

7.2 Money transmission is separate from the choice of asset

Regardless of which stablecoin is used: moving money for other people, across a border, for a fee, is money transmission — regulated state-by-state in the US (Money Transmitter Licenses) and federally (FinCEN MSB registration), with equivalent categories in the EU and elsewhere.

7.3 Chosen strategy: partner-first

Rather than Nivix obtaining its own Money Transmitter Licenses — realistically a lengthy, high-cost process for an early-stage company VERIFY current timeline/cost — Nivix contracts with a partner that already holds the relevant licenses to perform the USDC-to-fiat conversion and the payout to the named recipient. The partner's license covers the regulated act in that jurisdiction; Nivix's role is technology, orchestration, and its own treasury.

7.4 What this strategy does not eliminate VERIFY

Partnering shifts risk; it does not remove the need for legal review. Confirm specifically that the chosen partner's license scope covers receiving USDC from Nivix's treasury and paying a named third-party recipient — not merely processing payments on behalf of the partner's own direct merchants. License scope varies materially by provider and must be checked against Nivix's exact use case before any contract is signed.

8. Non-Negotiable Rules

These hold regardless of jurisdiction, corridor, or which specific partners are chosen. None of them should ever be reversed for convenience.

  1. No self-issued token labeled as, or standing in for, a real currency.
  2. No customer wallet holds, buys, or burns any crypto asset, at any point in the flow.
  3. No funding of stablecoin purchases with LRS or other outward-remittance-restricted money.
  4. No treasury private key without multisig or MPC and institutional custody.
  5. No commingling of customer funds still in transit with Nivix's own operating capital.
  6. No cross-border leg performed by any entity — Nivix or a partner — without a confirmed, current license or authorization covering that specific act, in that specific jurisdiction.

Appendix A — Glossary

FEMA
Foreign Exchange Management Act — India's core law governing cross-border currency transactions.
LRS
Liberalized Remittance Scheme — the FEMA facility allowing resident individuals to remit money abroad for permitted purposes; explicitly excludes purchasing virtual digital assets.
FIU-IND
Financial Intelligence Unit-India — the body virtual-digital-asset businesses must register with as reporting entities under PMLA.
PMLA
Prevention of Money Laundering Act — India's core anti-money-laundering law.
IFSC / IFSCA
International Financial Services Centre (GIFT City, Gujarat) and its regulator, the International Financial Services Centres Authority — treated as a non-resident jurisdiction for FEMA purposes.
VDA
Virtual Digital Asset — India's regulatory term covering cryptocurrencies and similar tokens.
MTL
Money Transmitter License — required state-by-state in the US to legally move money on behalf of others.
MSB
Money Services Business — the FinCEN federal registration category covering money transmitters in the US.
GENIUS Act
US federal legislation establishing a regulatory framework for "payment stablecoins," requiring 1:1 reserve backing and regular audits.
Multisig / MPC custody
Custody models requiring multiple independent approvals (multisig) or distributed key computation (MPC) to move funds — the alternative to a single private key controlling an entire treasury.
Payment Aggregator–Cross Border
A candidate RBI regulatory category for entities facilitating cross-border payments; exact current name and requirements flagged for verification in this document.
